\begin{abstract}
Calculators such as the BA II Plus Professional are used by actuaries to make critical decisions. Consequently, formal verification of the underlying mathematics is important.

The Annuity Equation is a staple of actuarial science and financial mathematics, relating a payment [PMT], present value [PV], future value [FV], interest rate [I/Y], and number of periods [N]. The BA II Plus Professional calculator allows a solution for any one of these five variables given the others, using keystrokes such as [CPT] [PMT].
Using the Lean proof assistant, we verify the unique solvability of the Annuity Equation for each variable under reasonable assumptions.

We also do the same for a Macaulay duration equation, with some help with Harmonic's ``mathematical superintelligence'' tool Aristotle.
\end{abstract}

\section{The annuity equation}
The Annuity Equation is often left implicit in textbooks on financial mathematics such as \cite{ChanTse2021_finmath}. Nevertheless it can be considered well known. It is as follows.
\[
\mathrm{[PV]} + \mathrm{[PMT]}\cdot \sum_{k=1}^{\mathrm{[N]}} (1 + (\mathrm{[I/Y]}/100))^{-k} + \mathrm{[FV]} \cdot (1 + \mathrm{[I/Y]}/100)^{-\mathrm{[N]}} = 0.
\]

In our formalization we assume that PV has the opposite sign of the common sign of PMT and FV, in order to avoid uniqueness issues stemming from Descartes' Rule of Signs, as in Norstr{\o}m's Criterion \cite{Norstrom1972IRR}.

This form gives the impression that division $x \mapsto x^{-1}$ is necessary, but the equation can also be stated in a future value perspective that makes sense over a ring:
\[
\mathrm{[PV]}\cdot (1 + \mathrm{[I/Y]}/100)^{\mathrm{[N]}} + \mathrm{[PMT]}\cdot \sum_{k=0}^{\mathrm{[N]-1}} (1 + (\mathrm{[I/Y]}/100))^{k} + \mathrm{[FV]} = 0.
\]
Of course, dividing (by 100) is indicative of a \emph{field}, but it is an artifact of social expectations and we can just as easily state everything in terms of an interest rate $i$, obtaining an equation for a \emph{ring}:
\[
\mathrm{[PV]}\cdot (1 + i)^{\mathrm{[N]}} + \mathrm{[PMT]}\cdot \sum_{k=0}^{\mathrm{[N]-1}} (1 +i)^{k} + \mathrm{[FV]} = 0.
\]

Another form is
\[
\alpha + \beta (1-v^n)/i + \gamma v^n = 0
\]
More symmetrically,
\[
\alpha v^{-n/2} + \beta (v^{-n/2}-v^{n/2})/i + \gamma v^{n/2} = 0
\]
In terms of $x=v^{-n/2}>0$,
\[
\alpha x + \frac{\beta (x-x^{-1})}i + \gamma x^{-1} = 0
\]
Solving for $x$,
\[
\alpha x^2 + \frac{\beta(x^2-1)}i + \gamma = 0
\]
\[
(\alpha + \beta/i)x^2 = \beta/i - \gamma
\]
\[
0 < x^2 = \frac{\beta/i-\gamma}{\beta/i+\alpha}
\]
This inequality is (unsurprisingly) also encountered in our Lean code.

Note that PMT/i is the perpetuity-equivalent principal, and we are saying it must be on the same side (left or right) of both FV and -PV.



Annuity feasibility inequality
or
Identifiability condition for maturity in level-payment cash flows: It’s not a “new inequality” in the sense of mathematics, but it is a very clean formulation of something practitioners assume without stating.

If PMT/i is above FV then it should mean that the coupon rate $r>i$ (the yield rate), i.e., the coupon rate is high.
But then PMT/i should also be above -PV, i.e., receiving these infintely many coupons is more valuable than what the actual contract is, i.e., receiving coupons for a while and then the redemption value.

Conversely, if PMT/i is below FV then the coupons are meager. Then PMT/i should also be below -PV, i.e., receiving these coupons forever is less valuable than the actual contract.

ChatGPT says it is essentially the comparison theorem for cash-flow streams hiding inside the annuity equation

Another way to look at it is that if PMT/i is above -PV, it means that the price of the bond is less than getting infinitely many coupons starting now; but since both of these deals involve getting the coupons at least until time $n$, it means getting the future value is less than getting infinitely many coupons from the point of view of the future time $n$.

Here's another way to look at it. If PMT/i > -PV, it means the bond (B) is worth less than the perpetuity (P). Now let's fast forward to time n. Both securities B and P have made the same payments so far. What's left? Only FV for the bond, and another PMT/i for P! So, by a monotonicity principle, PMT/i > FV as well.

\emph{If a perpetuity dominates a finite bond today, then it must also dominate the bond’s redemption value once all common coupons have been stripped away.}

In other words, if $C_t$ and $D_t$ are infinite cash flows streams and $C_t=D_t$ for all $t< t_0$, and $PV(C)>PV(D)$, and
$C^{(t)}$ is the security where all payments before $t$ are removed, then $PV(C^{(t_0)})>PV(D^{(t_0)}$ as well.


The Annuity Equation has apparently been implemented in financial calculators since their introduction half a century ago (June 13, 1976 for the original Texas Instruments Business Analyst \cite{TI-CL207A-1977}).
Here we formalize the fact you can solve for any of these five variables given the other four. This is rather explicit for PV, FV, PMT, and N, but requires Newton's method for I/Y (interest per year). However, our proof is mathematical and does not concern itself with computability. It instead sticks to mathematical existence and uniqueness.

\subsection{Some sample lemmas from the beginning of the project}
\begin{lemma}\label{sub_ne_zero_of_ne}
	\lean{sub_ne_zero_of_ne}
	\href{https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Algebra/Group/Basic.html#sub_ne_zero_of_ne}
	{\texttt{sub\_ne\_zero\_of\_ne}}
	
	If $a\ne b$ then $a-b\ne 0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{sum_pow}
	\lean{sum_pow}
	\leanok
	\uses{sub_ne_zero_of_ne}
	Given $n \in \mathbb N$ and $x \in \mathbb R$ with $x \ne 1$, we have
    $\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} x^i = \frac{x^n - 1}{x - 1}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{sum_pow_interest}
	\lean{sum_pow_interest}
	\leanok
	\uses{sum_pow}
	Given $n\in\mathbb N$ and $i\in\mathbb R$ with $i \ne 0$ and $1 + i \ne 0$,
  we have $\sum_{k=0}^n (1+i)^{-k}-1= \frac{1 - (1 + i)^{-n}}i$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{id_mul_geom_sum}
\lean{id_mul_geom_sum}
\leanok
Given $x\in\mathbb R$ with $x\ne 1$ and $n\in\mathbb N$, we have
\[
	\sum_{k=0}^n k \cdot x^k =
	(x \cdot (n \cdot x^{n + 1} - ((n + 1) \cdot x^n) + 1))/(x - 1)^2.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{sum_Icc_succ_eq_sum_range}
	\lean{sum_Icc_succ_eq_sum_range}
	\leanok
	Given $f:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$ and $n\in\mathbb N$, we have
\[
	f(0) + \sum_{k=1}^n f(k) = \sum_{k=0}^n f(k).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{id_mul_geom_sum₁}
	\lean{id_mul_geom_sum₁}
	\leanok
	\uses{sum_Icc_succ_eq_sum_range,id_mul_geom_sum}
Given $x\in\mathbb R$ with $x \ne 1$ and $n\in \mathbb N$, we have
\[
	\sum_{k=1}^n k \cdot x^k =
	  (x \cdot (n \cdot x ^ {n + 1} - ((n + 1) \cdot x ^ n) + 1)) / (x - 1) ^ 2.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{definition}\label{annuity.geom_sum}
	\lean{annuity.geom_sum}
	\leanok
	$\mathrm{geom\_sum}$ is the function $g : \mathbb N \to \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ given by
	$g(n,v)=
	  \sum_{k=1}^n v ^ k$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}\label{annuity.a}
	\lean{annuity.a}
	\leanok
	\uses{annuity.geom_sum}
	The present value of annuity function $a : \mathbb N \to \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$
	is defined by
	\[
		a_{\overline n|i}=g(n, (1 + i)^{-1})
	\] with $g$ as in Definition \ref{annuity.geom_sum}.
\end{definition}


\subsection{Lemmas about correctness of CPT key}
Under suitable conditions (see Theorem \ref{annuity_equation_unique_solvability}) we have the following five lemmas.

\begin{definition}\label{CPT_PV}
	\lean{CPT_PV}
	\leanok
	Defines a function to compute PV.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}\label{CPT_PMT}
	\lean{CPT_PMT}
	\leanok
	\uses{annuity.a}
	Defines a function to compute PMT, as
	\verb!(-PV - FV * (1 + IY / 100)⁻¹ ^ N) / annuity.a N (IY / 100)!.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}\label{CPT_IY}
	\lean{CPT_IY}
	\leanok
	Defines a function to compute IY.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}\label{CPT_N}
	\lean{CPT_N}
	\leanok
	Defines a function to compute N, as
	\verb!  (log ((PV * (IY / 100) + PMT) / (PMT - FV * (IY / 100)))) / (log (1 + IY / 100)⁻¹)!.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}\label{CPT_FV}
	\lean{CPT_FV}
	\leanok
	\uses{annuity.a}
	Defines a function to compute FV, as
	\verb!(- PV - PMT * (annuity.a N (IY / 100))) / ((1 + IY/100)⁻¹ ^ N)!.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{PMT_eq_CPT_PMT}
	\lean{PMT_eq_CPT_PMT}
	\leanok
	\uses{CPT_PMT}
$PMT$ equals $CPT PMT$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{lemma}\label{IY_eq_CPT_IY}
	\lean{IY_eq_CPT_IY}
	\leanok
	\uses{CPT_IY}
$IY$ equals $CPT IY$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{lemma}\label{PV_eq_CPT_PV}
	\lean{PV_eq_CPT_PV}
	\leanok
	\uses{CPT_PV}
$PV$ equals $CPT PV$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{lemma}\label{FV_eq_CPT_FV}
	\lean{FV_eq_CPT_FV}
	\leanok
	\uses{CPT_FV}
$FV$ equals $CPT FV$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{lemma}\label{N_eq_CPT_N}
	\lean{N_eq_CPT_N}

\end{lemma}


The following theorem summarizes these results:
\begin{theorem}\label{annuity_equation_unique_solvability}
	\lean{annuity_equation_unique_solvability}
	\leanok
	\uses{PMT_eq_CPT_PMT, IY_eq_CPT_IY, PV_eq_CPT_PV, FV_eq_CPT_FV, N_eq_CPT_N}
	Given real numbers $IY>0$, $PMT\ge 0$, $PV<0$, $FV>0$ and a natural number $N \ge 1$,
	satisfying the annuity equation, with 
	$(FV \cdot (IY / 100) - PMT \ne 0$, we can compute each of the five variables uniquely from the other four.
\end{theorem}



\section{Macaulay duration}

One of our main results is that if $D < 1 + 1 / i$ then we can recover $n$ (maturity) from $D$ (Macaulay duration \cite{Macaulay1938Problems}) and $r$ and $i$.

Actually $d < 1 + 1 / i$ follows from $r > i$.
If $r < i$ then we can only if $d < 1 + 1 / i$, which indeed does not hold in the graph in Chan and Tse
\cite[Figure 8.3 on page 251]{ChanTse2021_finmath}.

% From \texttt{AriExtremum.lean} and Chan and Tse figure,
When $r<i$ and $d>1+1/i$ (``deep discount'') there is a unique local extremum
and we cannot infer $n$ from $D$ except at the extremum.

\begin{tabular}{ l l l}
			  & $r<i$											& $r > i$	\\
$d < 1 + 1/i$ &	can recover $n$			& can recover $n$\\
$d > 1 + 1/i$ &	cannot recover $n$		& (impossible)\\
\end{tabular}

Aristotle \cite{achim2025aristotleimolevelautomatedtheorem} provided a single proof that $d<1+1/i$ suffices,
without doing a case split on whether $r<i$.

\begin{lemma}\label{AriMagic.exists_pos_root_of_limits}
	\lean{AriMagic.exists_pos_root_of_limits}
	\leanok
If a continuous function starts positive at 0 and tends to a negative limit, it has a positive root.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{AriMagic.unique_solution_n}
	\lean{AriMagic.unique_solution_n}
	\leanok
	Given $D>0, i>0, r>0$, with $D < 1 + 1/i$, there is a unique solution for the maturity $N$
	in the duration equation with duration $D$, yield rate $i$, and coupon rate $r$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{theorem}
	\label{eq_CPT_I_of_D}
	\lean{eq_CPT_I_of_D}
	\leanok
	If $D>1$, $n \ge 2$, and $r > 0$ then we can uniquely
	compute the yield rate $i > -1$ from the duration equation.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}
	\label{eq_CPT_N_of_D}
	\lean{eq_CPT_N_of_D}
	\leanok
	\uses{AriMagic.unique_solution_n,id_mul_geom_sum₁,AriMagic.exists_pos_root_of_limits}
	
Given $n\in\mathbb N$ with $n>1$ and $i,d,r$ positive real numbers,	 with  $d < 1 + 1 / i$,
 if the duration equation holds for $n,i,r,d$ then $n$ is uniquely determined,
 and given by the Lean function \verb!CPT_N_from_D!.
\end{theorem}

\section{Future work}
	The actuarial and financial mathematics literature is vast (see for example \cite{MR2500627}) and we have only laid some of the groundwork. A general continuation would be to add stochastic components. Related initiatives include formalization of Brownian motion in Lean \cite{degenne2025formalizationbrownianmotionlean} and, tangentially, formalization of smart contracts \cite{10.1145/3700601}.

	A very concrete next task would be to simply clarify to what extent two of our six variables can be computed from the other four:
	For instance, can yield rate and maturity be computed from present value, future value, Macaulay duration, and coupon rate?
